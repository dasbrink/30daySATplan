Is 30 Days Enough Time to Study for SAT Math?

By Dr. Brink, Ph.D., 32 years teaching mathematics · October 28, 2026

For most students, yes, if the 30 days are spent on the right things. A month of steady work, about half an hour on most days, is enough to fix the handful of mistakes that cost the most points. What a month usually cannot do is rebuild years of shaky basics from scratch. Where you start, and what kind of mistakes you make, decides how far 30 days takes you.

Students ask me this every fall, usually when the test is already a few weeks away and the summer plan never happened. Here is how I think about it, and what the numbers say.

What the numbers say

On my free practice site, freepracticeSAT.com, I looked at the full-length math tests students took between August and October. Students who started between 450 and 640 gained about 80 points on average from their first test to their best one. Most of them took all their tests within a few days, so that is roughly what a short burst of practice can do.

One student practiced steadily instead. They started at 570, were at 690 eight days later, scored between 700 and 730 for most of the next two weeks, and reached 780 on day 22. That is one student, not a promise, but it shows what a month of steady work can look like. I wrote about that student's path in more detail in how to go from 550 to 700 in SAT math.

College Board has a larger study. Among nearly 250,000 students, those who spent 20 hours on official practice gained 115 points on average from the PSAT to the SAT, about twice as much as students who did not. That was the whole SAT, not just math, and it shows a link rather than proof. But notice the number: 20 hours. Thirty days at 40 minutes a day is 20 hours.

Who 30 days works best for

Students losing points to slips and a few weak topics

If you are scoring somewhere around 550 to 700, most of your lost points probably come from a small number of mistakes you keep repeating, plus a few topics you never really learned. A month is a good amount of time for that. The trick is finding those mistakes in the first few days instead of the last few.

Students who have not studied at all yet

If you have never prepared for the SAT, the first weeks bring the quickest gains: learning the format, the timing and the built-in Desmos calculator is worth points on its own.

Students with shaky basics

If you are scoring below about 500, the trouble usually starts with skills from earlier grades: fractions, negative numbers, solving simple equations. Those sit under dozens of SAT questions. A month helps a lot, but it may not be the whole job. Start with the free Foundations course, four days of quick checks on exactly those skills, and if your test date allows it, give yourself longer than 30 days.

How I would spend the month

The order matters more than the hours. This is the outline the 30-day plan follows:

DaysWhat happens
Day 1 and 2A full diagnostic test, then a review of exactly which mistakes and skills cost you points.
Days 3 to 10Algebra and Advanced Math first, the two areas with the most questions, then Data Analysis and Geometry.
Days 11 and 12A second diagnostic and review, to see what has changed.
Days 13 to 22Your own weakest skills, then a second pass through the content areas.
Days 23 to 26A third diagnostic, a review, and two more days on what is still weak.
Days 27 to 30One full-length test a day, to build speed and stamina for test day.

Seven full-length tests in all, about 30 minutes a day on the other days.

Whatever you use, three things make a month work:

  1. Find your mistakes first. Take a full test in the first day or two and look at why you missed each question, not just which ones.
  2. Work every day, not every Saturday. Thirty minutes on most days does more than a long session once a week. I wrote about that in the 20-mile march.
  3. Check yourself every week or so. Take another full test and see whether the same mistakes still show up. If they do, slow down on them. If they don't, move on.

What 30 days will not do

Thirty days will not fix a problem you never look at. Students who spend the month taking one test after another without reviewing their mistakes usually end where they started, give or take the normal 20 or 30 points that practice scores bounce. And a month is not enough to cover every topic from the beginning, so do not try. Spend the time on the topics your own tests point to.

If your SAT is closer than about two weeks, a full plan does not fit. Take a practice test, go over your mistakes carefully, practice working on paper, and get some sleep.
