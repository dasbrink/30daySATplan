Negative and Fractional Exponents

SAT Math · Advanced Math · Updated October 2026

Two facts carry the whole topic

A negative exponent means take the reciprocal: $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$. It never makes the number negative.

A fractional exponent is a root: $a^{\frac{m}{n}} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m$. The bottom of the fraction is the root and the top is the power.

These questions reward students who know two definitions cold. Everything else is the ordinary exponent rules you already use. When I see a student miss one, it is almost always because $2^{-3}$ became $-8$ or because they took the power before the root and got stuck with a huge number.

Why the definitions make sense

I never ask students to memorize these as rules from nowhere. Write the powers of $2$ in a column: $2^3 = 8$, $2^2 = 4$, $2^1 = 2$. Each step down the exponent drops by $1$ and the value is cut in half. Keep going and the pattern has no choice: $2^0 = 1$, $2^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $2^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{4}$. A negative exponent is just the pattern continuing.

Fractional exponents come from the power rule. Since $\left(9^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^2 = 9^1 = 9$, the number $9^{\frac{1}{2}}$ must be something that squares to $9$, which is $3$. So $a^{\frac{1}{2}}$ is the square root, $a^{\frac{1}{3}}$ is the cube root, and so on. Once that clicks, a question like $8^{\frac{2}{3}}$ stops looking strange.

Take the root first. $27^{\frac{2}{3}}$ is $3^2 = 9$. Squaring first gives $729$, and you still have to find its cube root.

Examples

A negative and a fractional exponent together

Evaluate $16^{-\frac{3}{4}}$.

  1. Deal with the negative first: $16^{-\frac{3}{4}} = \dfrac{1}{16^{\frac{3}{4}}}$.
  2. The fourth root of $16$ is $2$, and $2^3 = 8$.
  3. So the value is $\dfrac{1}{8}$.

Rewriting a radical as a power

Which expression is equivalent to $\sqrt[3]{x^2} \cdot \sqrt{x}$ for $x > 0$?

  1. Write each root as a power: $x^{\frac{2}{3}} \cdot x^{\frac{1}{2}}$.
  2. Multiplying means adding exponents: $\dfrac{2}{3} + \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{7}{6}$.
  3. The answer is $x^{\frac{7}{6}}$. Multiplying the exponents, which gives $x^{\frac{1}{3}}$, is the trap.

Solving with a fractional exponent

If $a^{-\frac{1}{2}} = \dfrac{1}{5}$, what is $a$?

  1. Flip both sides: $a^{\frac{1}{2}} = 5$.
  2. Square both sides: $a = 25$.

What you will see

  • Evaluate a number. Something like $32^{\frac{3}{5}}$ or $\left(\dfrac{1}{9}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}$. Root first, then power, then flip if negative.
  • Match an equivalent expression. Rewrite every root as a fractional power, combine with the exponent rules, and compare.
  • Solve for a base or an exponent. Rewrite both sides with the same base when you can, then set the exponents equal.
  • Simplify a fraction of powers. Move negative exponents across the fraction bar and they turn positive.

Mistakes I see often

  • Making the number negative. $3^{-2}$ is $\dfrac{1}{9}$, a small positive number. The negative is about position, not sign.
  • Root and power swapped. In $a^{\frac{m}{n}}$ the bottom number is the root. $8^{\frac{2}{3}}$ is the cube root squared, not the square root cubed.
  • Multiplying exponents that should be added. $x^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot x^{\frac{1}{3}} = x^{\frac{5}{6}}$. You only multiply exponents for a power of a power.
  • Flipping the exponent instead of the base. $4^{-\frac{1}{2}}$ is $\dfrac{1}{2}$, not $4^2$.
