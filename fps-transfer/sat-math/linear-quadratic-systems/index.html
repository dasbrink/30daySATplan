Linear-Quadratic Systems on the SAT

SAT Math · Advanced Math · Updated October 2026

These systems come up in the second module, as plain equations and as graphs. Desmos makes the plain ones quick: graph both and click the crossing points. Questions with a constant like $c$ or $k$ still need the algebra.

How to solve them

Solve a system with a line and a parabola by substitution. Set the two expressions for $y$ equal to each other, move everything to one side, and solve the quadratic. Each $x$ you find gives one solution point, and you put it back into the line to get its $y$.

A line and a parabola can cross twice, touch once, or not meet at all. If a question asks how many solutions there are, look at the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ of the combined quadratic. Positive means two, zero means one, and negative means none.

The parabola $y = x^2$ and the line $y = x + 6$ cross at $(-2, 4)$ and $(3, 9)$. Each crossing point is a solution of the system.

Worked examples

Example 1: two crossing points

Solve $y = x^2$ and $y = x + 6$.

  1. Set equal: $x^2 = x + 6$, so $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.
  2. Factor: $(x - 3)(x + 2) = 0$, so $x = 3$ or $x = -2$.
  3. Put each into the line: $(3, 9)$ and $(-2, 4)$. Those are the two crossing points in the figure.

Example 2: a tangent line

For what value of $c$ do $y = x^2 + c$ and $y = 2x$ have exactly one solution?

  1. Set equal: $x^2 - 2x + c = 0$.
  2. One solution means the discriminant is $0$: $(-2)^2 - 4(1)(c) = 0$.
  3. $4 - 4c = 0$, so $c = 1$. The line just touches the parabola at $(1, 2)$.

Example 3: a horizontal line

How many solutions do $y = x^2 - 4x + 7$ and $y = 3$ have?

  1. Set equal: $x^2 - 4x + 4 = 0$, which is $(x - 2)^2 = 0$.
  2. Only $x = 2$ works, so there is exactly one solution.
  3. That makes sense: the vertex of the parabola is $(2, 3)$, and the line $y = 3$ touches it right there.

Where students lose points

Forgetting the second solution. A quadratic usually gives two $x$ values. Each one is a separate crossing point.

Finding $x$ and stopping. Remember that a solution is a point, with both an $x$ and a $y$. If the question asks for $y$, put $x$ back into the line.

Using the parabola's discriminant alone. Combine the two equations first. The discriminant that matters is of the combined quadratic.

Testing a point in only one equation. $(2, 4)$ is on $y = x^2$, but that doesn't make it a solution of the system.

Sign slips when moving terms. $x^2 = x + 6$ becomes $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.

Hard
In the system $y = x^2$ and $y = k$, $k$ is a constant. For which values of $k$ does the system have no real solutions?

  • C$k < 0$

Answer: C. $x^2 = k$ has no real solutions when $k$ is negative, because a square is never negative. The line $y = k$ is then below the vertex of the parabola. With $k = 0$ there is one solution.

Answer: A. $2x^2 - 3x - 2 = x + 4$ gives $2x^2 - 4x - 6 = 0$, or $x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0$. So $x = 3$ or $x = -1$. With $a = 3$, $b = 3 + 4 = 7$. Choice B is the $y$-value for $x = -1$.
